# Reflective Essay

The results of this project are mostly negative and it's a little disappointing. Though I can reflect on it because the negative results trace back to decisions I made long before any experiment was run.

The project began in a single direction, built around generators, consumers, and storage, with the battery acting as the smart device that balances profit against its own health. The motivation then became adapting to scenarios that are not known at training time.

## Strengths

What I do still stand behind is the motivation. It addresses a real problem in today's energy storage industry, where assets are exposed to shocks and changing conditions.

The results are also not completely negative. The dyna (Sutton, 1991) results are positive, though under one condition, which is that the world model has to pass the calibration gate first. When it does, an agent trained on a single year of observed prices comes close to what a model-free agent achieves with an unlimited supply of real ones, because the model turns that year into far more training days than the year itself contains. The agent needs more history, but quality plays a crucial role.

The part of the work I am most satisfied with is the effort that went into establishing why each result came out the way it did. The obvious explanation for dyna's margin is that it simply takes more gradient steps, so the comparison was built to rule that out. Replay trains on the identical year for as many epochs as it wants and still finishes far behind, which leaves the number of distinct training days as the thing doing the work. The negative results were handled the same way. The nightly refit loop fails because incremental refitting drives the market model out of calibration within three nights, weeks before the shift it was meant to catch, and the physics-informed network (PINN) (Raissi et al., 2019)  loses to a plain network because an ablation against deliberately wrong physics shows its residual carries no information at small data budgets. A negative result with a named mechanism is a positive find rather than just a run that didn't work.

Though I am careful about how far that goes. The gate's contribution is based on the intuition that the training data generated must be similar to the real data, but because no policy was ever trained inside a model the gate rejected, that claim is not proven. So every dyna number I quote comes from the one draw that passed.

## Weaknesses

At the start I spent too much time trying to devise something interesting and complicated out of the things I wanted to work with. I was too ambitious about what the project should be, and the direction I settled on was never concrete enough, so it went through many refinements. The project became an amalgamation of different techniques and approaches, each of which introduced its own problems and challenges, and together they led to poor results. I should have been more decisive about what to work on and kept it simple and focused on the things I wanted to work with. The project would then have shaped itself once the foundation was right.

The clearest weakness in how I worked was that I did not ask why something worked when it did. The market model has to pass a calibration gate before it is allowed to train a policy, and at a budget of 365 observed days it passed, so I treated that as settled and built the rest of the project on that configuration. Only much later, when I repeated the same fit over five independent draws of the year, did I find that it passes once in five. The setting I had been treating as the working case was a favourable draw, and everything downstream had inherited that luck. The direction survived the check, because the dyna vs replay ordering still held at every training seed once the history was pinned, but the headline margin fell by about half. A single passing run tells you that a method can work, it isn’t flawless, and the sweep that separates those two costs a few hours of compute. I would rather have paid that in the second month than in the final weeks.

## Theory and Practical Work

Building the simulator taught me a great deal about the energy market and about battery dynamics, and that knowledge went directly into the environment and tools the models train in. Because I have a lot of programming experience, the simulation itself was not the hard part, it was designing the experiments and implementing the training techniques that took the most time.

The stage is a battery that has to balance profit against its own health, so something has to model both the market and the battery physics. A PINN is the intuitive choice for the battery half, because charging and discharging obey laws I already know, and writing those laws into the model should pay off exactly where samples are scarce, which is the sample efficiency problem I set out to tackle in the first place. It did not work out that way. A plain MLP won at every budget below 500 samples, and by widening margins as the data thinned. What decides whether a physics prior helps is not whether the system is physical, it is whether the function is hard to learn from the data available. The battery map is three inputs to three outputs and smooth, so an unconstrained network interpolates it from a handful of points and there is nothing left for the prior to contribute.

## Further Work

The most substantive next step is repairing the nightly refit. In the adaptation experiment the market shifts partway through the deployment, and the agent refits its market model every night to keep up. Each refit predicts the newest day well, but the days it generates drift wider than the real market's by the third night on calm data and a month before the shift arrives, and they never come back, so the agent spends the deployment training on spreads that do not exist. Checking the refit each night and freezing it when it fails is not a repair either, since it would freeze on night three and leave me with the frozen model I was already comparing against. What is needed is a refit that takes in new days without forgetting the year it was fitted on, and refitting from scratch on the pooled history each night is where I would start.

On the battery side, the question I would keep is not about physics priors. A learned battery can predict what cannot happen, such as more energy coming out than went in, and an agent training inside it will find those mistakes and use them. Clamping the outputs to physically possible values stops that, and any network can carry a clamp. Whether the clamp on its own is enough is the experiment to run, and my study cannot say, because I only measured prediction error against a fixed market and never trained an agent inside a learned battery.

The furthest reaching direction is to drop the assumption that the battery is too small to affect prices. Three things depend on it: the agent can train on whole imagined days because nothing it does changes the prices it sees next, the benchmark is a single plan against a fixed list of prices, and the gate checks invented days against a market that sits still. A battery large enough to bid would break all three at once, so this is a limit on how far my results reach rather than a next experiment. The case I would study is a middling asset, big enough to change which generator sets the price on scarce evenings but not on ordinary days, since that is where most of the arbitrage money is.

## What I Would Have Done With More Time

The first thing I would have done is run a policy inside the models the gate rejected. The experiment is the same SAC, with the same settings and the same number of imagined days, inside the 90-day, 365-day and 1095-day ensembles, evaluated on the real simulator over the same twenty paired episodes. Every arm invents as many days as it is asked for, so the amount of training data is identical and the only thing that changes is how closely the generator resembles the market. The 1095-day arm is the sharp one, since it is fitted on three times as much real history as the arm that passed, so if its policy comes out worse then dyna needs the quality of the fit and not the quantity of the record, which is currently something I infer from gate verdicts and not from any policy. I expect both rejected arms to lose clearly, and if they do not, the gate is ceremony and what carries dyna is simply having many distinct days to train on, whatever they look like. A model that fails the gate is never saved and the training script refuses to run without one that passed, so the arm needs a deliberate override at both ends, and by the time I understood that the gate's own contribution was the weakest link in my argument there was no time left for three more training runs.

The second is the sweep I did run, applied to everything else. The seed work arrived late and covers only the budget experiment at 365 days. The robustness table, the adaptation deployments and the feasibility measurements each rest on a single training run, so their numbers carry the variance across market weeks but not the variance across training runs, and the fixed-history sweep showed that the second is not small. Dyna spans 66.2 to 72.7 percent of the hindsight optimum across five training seeds and replay spans 46.6 to 59.6. I do not think any of those orderings would flip, because the gaps are wide, but I cannot say so from the evidence I have, and reporting a spread I measured in one place while leaving it unmeasured in three others is the part of the paper I am least comfortable with.

Third, more points on the data budget. Three budgets are enough to show that one year passed and that its neighbours failed, and not enough to say whether that is a window with edges or an accident of where I happened to sample. Budgets at 180, 270, 540 and 730 days would have covered it. Doing it properly means several independent draws at every budget, because the draw matters as much as the budget does, and that is genuinely expensive.

Finally, the simulator records which technology is dispatched every hour, so the generation mix during the hours the agent charges and discharges is already contained in results I have run. Measuring it would say how much the profit-maximising policy trades in carbon when considering the benefits of a trading battery. It needs no training and no new experiment, only a script over episodes that already exist.

## Legal, Social, Ethical and Sustainability Issues

The libraries I used are all permissively licensed and I've complied with them so legally there are no issues. Because the project doesn't interact with society there are no social issues. There were no human participants and no real data of any kind, so ethics approval and data protection never came up. That would change if I fitted the simulator to real demand data, since household consumption shows when people are home.

The project also took a lot of compute trading GPU hours instead of years of cycling a real battery. There's an irony in a project about storing energy efficiently using a fair amount of it to get there.
